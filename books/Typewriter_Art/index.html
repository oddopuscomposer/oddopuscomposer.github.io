Typewriter Art

Berrie Tullett  ·  2014

2025-12  ·  2026-01  · 

2025-12  ·  semiotics   type  ·  ascii   fine-art


Continuing my exploration of ASCII art and semiotic glyph exploration, I read another book I had sitting on my shelf for a number of years to gain an appreciation of the history of recontextualization of text character as mark and gesture in both entity placeholder and literal game world architecture and cartography.

Typewriter Art: A Modern Anthology by Berrie Tullett looks at the full history from the standardization of the machine to modern practitioner all utilizing the typewriter against its initial prose intention and how the technology freed people to experiment as a tool for a new medium of fine art, illustration, and ergodic text block explorations. Throughout the sections are interviews with various typewriter artists and curators.

I admit many parts were particularly inspiring and had humorous history recounting from Olivetti predating Apple by having a rainbow of colours that iMacs almost reference in their candy coloured plastics (pg 17) to the 1950’s many different colour ribbons, to the art appreciation that I felt for Gomringer’s Silence 1953 piece. Which is a piece that doesn’t exist - the poem is blank. I suppose this is the natural progression, perhaps from an artist who previously worked with one-word poems, but in this case, the artwork’s meaning is derived precisely from its absence. Kind of like the coolest indie rock band is the one I just made up and never released any music, 2007 Alix, eat your heart out. Shout out to Oral Tetris, Yesterdays Squash Blossoms, and Pocket. Maybe someday.

I want to point out how inspirational the visual aspect of Steve McCaffery’s 1970s two-panel piece Carnival, was in this read. While it is initially an exploration of concrete poetry, I am much more interested in the beautiful cartographic result, which is very satisfying to me as an ink and paper Dwarf Fortress with some interesting playing of glyph scale. He can be seen resonating to a potentially kind of similar vibe on page 61, “McCaffery says it as ‘essentially a cartographic project; a repudiation of linearity in writing and the search for an alternative syntax in “mapping””. Though if you want a true trip, you can also experience it as he reads the concrete poem.

Author Will Self has this banger of a quote: “The god of the computer is a forgiving one; the god of the typewriter is not” – basically noting that typewriters bring creativity and non-computer users have to do more thinking in their head than on the screen page. There is a memory and permanence to the decisions being made. This is similarly expressed by curators Simon Butler mentions the aspects of typewriters living in tangible mediums -the residue the pressure of keys strikes - they reveal a historical event, time, moment, traces of the application of its creation and the papers memory (pg 13) and similarly as the Curator Angie Butler puts it in the book introduction (and I’m paraphrasing) “it allows a place to appreciate and celebrate mistakes. mistakes allow crossroads so to decide to start again or change your mind about the work. a sense of history is imbued in a typewriter (work)”. I can relate a bit to this as a musician, as a lot of midi instrumentation applications do not have velocity pressure in the time of playing, missing out on some key or vital parts of the music creation’s expression and I wonder how much of that information is missed when I type an email, when I draft a blog or write a creative document. Page 120 Simon Gomes has a wonderfully lovely quote that exemplifies a bit of this feeling: “I love the serendipitous hit and miss of the letters on paper.. I like the idea of kissing the paper lightly, or metaphorically hitting it with a hammer - an unpredictability not afforded by digital devices.” The joy of enacting through human embodiment.

In the spirit of rectifying my angst from AI sludge, artist Alan “Riddle felt that an artist’s engagement with technology was the only way to humanize the modern world and overcome the ‘deadening’ effect of mechanization” (page 47). The joy of being informed by our process to make and the decisions we make along the way is the fiction we learn from.

Another interesting tidbit that got me thinking was Peter Kubelk Typewriter scores for the film from 1950. The Austrian experimental filmmaker was ousted by the scandal of his film, Schwechater, and while in Sweden he did not have access to film equipment, he was so inspired by how typewriters presented information in a linear fashion akin to the film rolls he was used to, he would cut out single lines of his work to create “paper films”. Talking about his process, he said “to create a completely black field, I had to type five different letters on the same spot”. Something interesting perhaps could be said here, around that it is diversity which creates rich density, eh?

Anyway, it was between these movements of contrasts that would generate his - well, film strip, I guess. And it makes me recall other sequential systems from scan lines of CRTs, to contrasted musical note systems from the hole punch mechanism of player pianos and chime boxes, to light reader synths triggering oscillators, to the old musical procedural systems like compositional dice games. This then lends to emergent systems of musicians of Steve Reich, Terry Riley, and Brian Eno, and the very abstract visual pieces as well, with musical composition graphics, given an aesthetic take on some sort of phenomenology and synesthesia betwixt.

One interview I’ll point out was the fascinating artist Allyson Strafella on pages 152-153. Where she details how she excavated marks she liked in drawings she had made, and identified 29 to be modified into a large typewriter. She had her chosen marks cast in solid and outline form since the typewriter could be outfitted with capitalization and lowercase options, given the type scale remained the same. This is quite interesting to me as she redefined essentially the tileset she uses to build. She created a new architecture conlang to build her works from her mark-making and rejected the Latin alphabet defaulted in the machine. Whereas prior, the most one might hope for was italics or the occasional unusual symbol.

It inspires me to continue exploring 1 bit glyph, including alternatives to the Latin alphabet. Most modern-day roguelikes have an ASCII option and a tileset (though minimal, usually 8x8, 16x16, or 24x24 pixels) for a more modern game literacy access. I do wonder about suspending from the ASCII set entirely, and for a new n that might be foreign but easier to project meaning on.

In the end, a surprisingly fruitful read for me that left a lot of interesting connections typing away in my head.
