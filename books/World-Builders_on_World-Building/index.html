World-Builders on World-Building

Mark J.P. Wolf  ·  2020

2025-11  ·  2026-03  · 

2025-11  ·  world-building  ·  game-development


A nice book of seven eclectic essays by various authors on world-building. Two which particularly resonated with me were the trifecta history of MUD presented by Richard A. Bartle and The Place of Culture, Society, and Politics in Video Game World-Building, an essay by Mark R. Johnson. Both are inspirational bookends, sitting as the second and sixth chapter focuses in the book and represent an origin to game development as a father of multiuser dungeons and a contemporary roguelike academic of game studies.

Richard A. Bartle’s trifecta historical recollection, The Making of MUD: Three Stories of Genesis, is a reflection on the overall origins of the creation of MUD from three perspectives (programmatic craft of systems, design of world building, and art-oriented).

MUD is short for multi-user dungeon and is an early fantasy role-playing game that ran through a server, allowing multiple players to join and play together, adding an exciting element to messaging real people, perpetuating a real-time game space for creative theater as players solved puzzles and smited enemies. Its inception started in 1978 by Roy Trubshaw, and by 1980 Bartle took over development, with the game becoming reachable over the network to users across the world by the early 80s. This became quite popular to the point that it spawned its own genre, similarly named (MUDs), with dozens of games in the format’s style from many creators. This was all pre the modern internet as we know it and was text based precursor to the modern massive multiplayer online (MMO) games that came decades after, like EverQuest and World of Warcraft (a game that has grossed over 9 billion dollars at this time).

Looking at the three perspectives:

Programmatically, in a very technical sense, the game system was based around high-level rewriting and keeping data binaries open to overwrite, while everyone used the same program (server) and eventually progressed to version 3 of MUD (aptly and awkwardly became known as MUD1), and content could be more easily managed and improved and built upon by referencing external documents while maintained with a universal syntax structure by the admins.

Design-wise, Bartle looked at MUD as initially providing a virtual stage for people to make their own objects and story drama, but it soon became clear they needed to make MUD more of an actual game, incorporating puzzles and characters, systems. Lives were added to soften permadeath, inspired by pinball machines 3 balls and additional ones you gain along the way. But despite the advanced life broken into subsequent hit points to allow a more exciting and unknown combat outcome, newly created characters were usually just extensions of the players themselves, whether surviving or reborn. This one problem that users did not seem to be role-playing bothered Bartle, and he thought perhaps players had to be shown that there was a shield to mockery and permission to role-play as a new avatar, making new decisions in-game.

I found this section exceptionally quaint and charming and rather progressive when he mentions the discussion around gender and implementation at the time and now: “Players didn’t believe they had permission to role-play. It was not part of the culture of the game, because it wasn’t part of the culture of the real-life society in which they lived. They needed to be shown that MUD offered them a cultural shield, so that they could pretend to be someone else without fear of mockery.” 56-57 “This meant that when I added to MUD the option for characters to be female, Polly was naturally going to be female. I could then play Polly as a cheerful, feisty young woman who was helpful but didn’t suffer fools gladly. The other players knew I was me; they also knew I was Polly; they knew how I played as Polly was not how I played as me; they knew that my playing as not-me was permissible; they realized that their playing as not-them was also permissible; they played as not-them. Thenceforth, people could and almost always did role-play in MUD. This freed them up to explore different facets of themselves, as intended.” 57

Lastly, from an aesthetic perspective, Bartle mentions that some system elements were inspired by his harsh class upbringing, locking him into societal roles. Despite his smarts and loving childhood, he looked at computers and video game character experience as a fairer measure of what a person did rather than the abstraction of birth into. While levels might mimic class, the key is that any player can actually rise through them. He looks at it as a political and artistic statement of their discontent with the social order of things.

Ultimately, quite an entertaining and insightful second essay in the book, so I’ll end with one more favorite quote from Bartle:

“When you make a game, you learn what it is you want to say and how to say it. Its mechanics are your language; its gameplay, your message.” 66

The second essay chapter I want to highlight is The Place of Culture, Society, and Politics in Video Game World-Building by Mark R. Johnson (Essay 6)

Johnson takes a look at what it takes to make a more realistic concept of a game world and why don’t designers/developers incorporate more focus on history, more reflected through cultural artefacts and systems, rather than an expositional voice (whether spoken or textual). Focusing less on player convenience, such as level design way finding and more thinking on how a world’s historicity is messy and layered with culturally distinct and diverse cultures.

So what does it take to make a world feel convincing, not shatter illusions, and feel permanent, like it is a real place within its own history? Its sociological world-building is made up of the macro and micro political, social, economic, and perhaps spatial elements influencing the NPC and its organization. This is reflected in items like clothing, metal, and architecture, societies like systems like governments, guilds, and religions, and spatial/geolocation aspects like dialects, fashion, trade interests, and naming conventions.

Before Johnson delves into a few given examples from his own creative roguelike Ultima Ratio Regum, which he has created a considerable system “as a means to creating fictional spaces with a deep sense of sociological verisimilitude.” he give a few mainstream insights with Soulsborn world crafting approach Paradox Interactive game like Crusader Kings, Victoria, and Stellaris - all inspiring examples of his bottom-up approach of having a player be immersed in a realistic world told through the artefacts and NPC action non-expositional often contains a messy histories stacked.

“The temporal reversal of how the world appears to have been built. To put this another way, it genuinely appears that long, complex, and contested sequences of events have led up to the present state of affairs, rather than a present state of affairs having been decided by the world-builder(s) and then historical events inserted to lead up to a predetermined endpoint.”

“These are worlds where the flow of ‘history’ and its impact on the present are just as messy and contested as they are in reality, yet just as visible for someone who invests the time in seeking out their signs and clues.”

Here, Johnson is referencing the Soulsbourne world, where the player explores crowded city streets and stairways built for giants, the designers are telling of history through architecture and artifacts, and the way NPCs might offhandedly respond to time or cultural understanding not immediately familiar to a player. This perpetuates the trivial into becoming meaningful; all the items and architecture suggest a world built and lived in - fought over - decayed - a history has happened and is happening, all of which can be missed or harnessed for narrative insight.

He goes into the nuances of how systems might simulate organic interactions, determining realistic outcomes from the simulated cultures in general and his own game UUR. This might be best reflected in his statements about how a system like religion might permeate outward push and pull against various systems at play:

“Every belief system has a procedurally generated holy book or series of books (which adherents might quote), a set of edicts and expectations, different moral codes, different styles of clothing, and different perspectives on how nonbelievers should be treated. A particularly zealous religion might be the most likely to send preachers to different cities (to potentially be encountered by the player) or send inquisitors throughout their own territory to find potential heretics.” -and- “For example, a religion might believe that heavy weapons such as maces should not be used because they do too much damage to human bodies (which are perhaps sacred?) and thus no soldiers following that religion will use these weapons.” “For example, a religion might believe that heavy weapons such as maces should not be used because they do too much damage to human bodies (which are perhaps sacred?) and thus no soldiers following that religion will use these weapons.”

I love this idea of situating a player in an unfamiliar world and how they might reflect that in their excursions, adventures, and their expeditional exhibitions through the hints of change and treatment of society. The idea of cultures reflecting on the player as a potential outsider, how can they tell? How do they treat outsiders? Do they have a history of outsiders like our player character? Will they be judged on their appearance, such as clothes, jewellery, or hair fashion? Why would the player be let into their home to rummage through their stuff? Should the player have a hometown, and how would we make that feel different and possibly exciting? I mean, what drives someone to roam the world? And how do these things of a biome, culture, player represented in the artefacts, architecture, governance, NPCs practices, etc.

It was quite an enjoyable essay, and as someone looking into diverse history and culture generations, as well as, admittedly, a roguelike fan who was familiar with UUR. I’ll end with a quote to the heart of what he is articulating:

“the goal has always been twofold: to create a world which is less obvious and less transparent than many fictional worlds, and to use bottom-up sociological world-building to build a planet which is not just a complex set of interwoven elements, but also different every time a player creates a new world – yet always one that is, hopefully, as deep as the one which came before it.”
