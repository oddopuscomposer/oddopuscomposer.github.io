The Weird And Eerie

Mark Fisher  ·  2016

2026-09  ·  2026-10  · 

2026-09  ·  semiotics  ·  media-analysis


This was my first Mark Fisher book, and I’m delighted by how approachable he is. His philosophical, literary, music, and film criticism never feels like a locked room. It’s also his last finished work. It came out in the UK in early 2017, and he died about a month later, at 48, before the worldwide release, so it’s often billed as posthumous. I came to this work sideways, initially inspired by his popularising of hauntology: the “lost futures” of the past that haunt the present.

Fisher postulates that “the weird is constituted by a presence - the presence of that which does not belong.” (61), while “The eerie, by contrast, is constituted by a failure of absence or by a failure of presence” (61). The weird could be constituted in an echo of the past playing back in the present, like as a ghostly apparition. The eerie manifests and percolates in the quiet of the empty streets at night in a city.

Suspense and speculation, he notes, aren’t essential to the weird (62). What is essential is the “opposition between the quotidian and the mysterious” (30), the friction of the mundane with the supernatural. It’s a fascination mixed with trepidation, something that not only repels but compels attention (17).

“The sense of wrongness associated with the weird - the conviction that this does not belong - is often a sign that we are in the presence of the new. The weird here is a signal that the concepts and frameworks which we have previously employed are now obsolete.” (13).

This brings to mind the intersection where the once familiar diminishes into the unknown to a group of people or a culture. Gen Z being introduced to text adventure games or forgotten media or tech, like cassette tapes, for me perhaps operating a laser disc. They become cursed artefacts absent of humans to a degree, but still traceable, unlike Stonehenge’s mysteries or the abandoned buildings’ histories, whose histories are los in the abandonment further from a browser search and no longer being made into memories.

The eerie, meanwhile, offers a “release from the mundane,” or a revealing of the obscured ordinary (13). Making me think of Marcel Duchamp’s Infrathin, the space between spaces that emerge. The sound of clothing through movement, lingering taste of smoke, a seat still warm.

I wonder if the eerie is a space that exists in a person’s mind? Or the gap, a void space of questions that now has to coexist in the shuffling of our current understandings. Perhaps this is way monsters left more undefined become much greater and intriguing in the fear around them. One might say The Blairwitch Project good, they didn’t show the witch, albiet by accident by scaring the actors so much. Stranger Things is far more eerie and… well strange the first few episodes, before we understand its a bipedal Little Shop of Horrors Aubrey II. Over the Garden Wall’s inevitable devourment of the forrest which claims us all eventually. It is ever waiting, but we can choose how we spend out time now in the meantime -(its a rock fact!). Abstract paintings work inthe same way, as do the storys we invent to fill in for the unexplainable larger variables of weather, chemicals, thermodynamics, and general emergent systems. Our apophenia is our minds’ pattern-seeking trying to minimise uncertainty, and while we reside in uncertainty, we experience a weird and eerie moment. Mark Fisher posits it’s not just this mystery of the unknown but requires alterity (104).

Fisher describes the eerie points to the unknown, the unfamiliar, this “outside” and the non-domestic absence of humans, though not necessarily their traces. This curiosity and mystery of the unknown: what produced such ruin? What entity? What emitted such an eerie cry? “The eerie is fundamentally tied up with questions of agency.” (11) What agent, if any, is here? Fisher ties this to capital and capitalist society - where money, an abstraction, holds exceptional influence compared to substantive entities. Alternatively, this also reminds me of horror game design, which is eerie through the reduction and rationing of resources, particularly of knowledge, to produce unease through restriction to increase the weight and feeling of risk with any player action.

This weird is not merely fantastic in an otherworldly exchange Unlike Narnia or THe Wizard of OZ, where an individual is changed by entering another world, here there is a reciprocity:the other world encroaches on and oppresses ours, things and aspects where they should not be but are. (19)

I like that Fisher mentions this, as the Necronomicon is only cited in HP Lovecraft’s works if thre was a “real” work, it would no longer emit the weird and eerie, as the illusion of it being ordinary writing would shatter any illusion. Leaving it in Limbo makes it seem more plausible, just lost or rare. House of Leaves works similarly in its hobnobbing of fake citations mixed amongst legit ones. This is the problem of the Stranger Things mystery: as soon as the threatening beast Demogorgon is introduced in an understood form, it becomes clear that its familiar aspects are no longer eerie, perhaps weird, but a level of fright and intrigue drains away. Compare that to the film The Mothman Prophecies; Mothman is never seen; it is an incomprehensible figure. We do not know why, how, or really what, and that is eerie and full of trepidation.

Perhaps we are but fragments and figments, and we can not help but change our nature when crossing into other worlds. Shall I see you on the other side?
