<b>General Thoughts</b>:
What I find alluring in this book is the range of approaches to the illustrative and textual practice: varying perspectives and scales, identification systems, taxonomies and classifications, connections, abstract and artistic routes. They are charming snapshots in frozen time mixed with the personal values and desires of the cartographer, no matter how fantastic or surreal. As contributor Hugh Brogan writes, we provide maps to give "concreteness to wholly imagined worlds" for text to be "verified" (p. 151).

Not just providing a verisimilitude to visualised landscape, Author Harmon similarly describes maps as a vehicle for our imagination, none more so than those who ignore mapping conventions: "We look at these maps, and our minds know just what to do: take the information and extrapolate from it a place where they can leap, play, gambol - without that distant province of our being, the body dragging them down" (pp. 10-11). She adds, "Of Course, part of what fascinates us when looking at a map is inhabiting the mind of its maker, considering that particular terrain of imagination overlaid with those unique contour lines of experience." (p. 11).

A map is a coded visual language that we, as cartographers, decide what to include or omit, and how to express it, so to articulate a perspective and organise our opinion of what is important.

There is a roguelike joy of setting the parameters for procedurally generated geography and being surprised by the delight of the resulting world, its map, its inhabit entities, its epithetic lore, all conjured and emerged from our algorithmic direction and fed further from our apophenia.

To dig deeper in the traditional roguelike percolation, how do we fit into this landscape? Starting from a roguelike dungeon map, fit ourselves into space? How relative are the oriented bias spaces Sara Ahmed describes in her book *Queer Phenomenology* when we reside and move about our top-down, monospaced, glyphed map of rooms, forests, and caves, situated amongst the dangers and treasure of the simulated world? How to wander or orient? 

Another contributor, Stephen S. Hall, defines orienteering as "to orientate is to hop back and forth between landscape and time, geography and emotion, knowledge and behaviour." (p. 15) This is an great quote which I feel coincides nicely with Elizabeths Freeman concept of Chronormativity (*Time Binds*) and Ahmed's bias space, supporting the queer sense of time can be personally, geographically bound. Name, pronoun, or projected orientation become geolocational, gridlocked to the family home, the workspace, groups of friends, and your own personal space. Within them, echoes still reverberate loudly until we make enough noise to overwrite the previous memory associations. Hall's words further intrigue this thought when he writes, "Like memory, geography is associative. In this process, I call orienting well all carry a personal atlas in our brains" (pp. 15-16).

This similarly resonates with a reading of contributor Katie Davis's description of how a memory becomes fixed and static, even with an ever-living map. People relocate, and the environment changes: an old tree that fell down, a neighbour's house named for a childhood memory of a tenant who long since left.

Anyway, plenty to ponder as I move on to my next read, *Videogame Architecture: Building Virtual Spaces* by Luke Pearson & Sandra Youkhana). I'll wrap up by mentioning I thought it was quaintly cute that Katherine Harmon ended the book with a map of her experiences, which led to her developing this book and drafting a fantastical map of those moments and the contributors to the book (*The River of Gratitude*, 2003).
