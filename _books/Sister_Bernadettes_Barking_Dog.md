Sister Bernadette's Barking Dog - The Quirky History and Lost Art of Diagramming Sentences

An enjoyable quick read on the history of sentence diagramming with some amusing autobiographical vignettes and nice perculations on a few prominent writers, and whether their style and skill might have been influenced by the presence (or lack of) sentence diagramming in their youthful educations. I particularly enjoyed some zingers by Gertrude Stein and Henry James, which I shall share below:

While I myself am a fan of commas, appreciating a good oxford variety and the enabling sense they give me for my perpetual garden sentencing on page 66, Gertrude Stein drops this absolute queen jazz “Commas are servile and have no life of their own”..."helping you along, holding your coat for you and putting on your shoes” or as Florey describes in her reading of Gertrude Steins grammer opinion she was now armed with the knowledge that commas are slavish, enfeebling little suck-ups“. I am sorry, commas, I do love you despite what Miss Gertrude Stein says.

<aside> <p>
“Don’t call me Miss Stein,” she once said. “Call me Gertrude Stein.” (66)
</p></aside>
I also vibe considerably with Henry James' artistic tempers -
* As Gore Vidal put it, James “believed that a work of art was never finished, merely abandoned.” (page 72) As someone who has projects that now have been going on for decades, never shared, so I feel you, Henry James. Mood.
* And, as James himself said in a letter to his niece (quoted by Leon Edel in his introduction to volume four of The Letters of Henry James), “I hate American simplicity. I glory in the piling up of complications of every sort.” (also page 72) I said I feel you, Mr. James. Seen.

This loquacious sesquipedalian is happy with ergodic and exploratory of meaningful complex poetics. Perhaps someday I'll try to read Joyce's Finnegan's Wake and change my mind. But like visual art, there is such space for all expressive prose, and we should encourage the diverse from direct to the obtuse, layered word with layered meanings.
Speaking of poetics, Florey has some lovely turns of phrase, such as early on in page 9, "We hung those sentences out like a wash, wrote them like lines of music, arranged them on a connecting web of veins and arteries until we understood every piece of them.".

One thing that was a bit strange is that there is this through line that pops up every now and again from chapter to chapter conservatism. Perhaps it's aged poorly given the current state of the world and shame of my homelands fascist progressions, but there are some weird takes that aged poorly in a time where literally American and UK misinformation attacks on various minorities and force strict heteronormativity, and at times push proto-fascist ideology. I can't tell if Florey is just pro-traditionalism and at times pokes some lite humour at political leaders like USA president George W. Bush, which might feel very liberally minded to the author and just reasonably factual to others.
It's an abundant issue with our "illiterate public figures,"! The dismal intelligence and celebrated bigotry are abhorrent, as well as absolutely loathsome. At the same time it gives very "old man yells at cloud" energy when talking about how students get to express themselves, that the 60's started the downfall of rigor, premarital abstinence, and respect of elders, or that political correctness is ruining language. A lot of these points are revisited at the end of the book on page 143.

I am at least, I am happy that we agree that it's probably a good thing that students don't get starkly abused daily by teachers. Though perhaps it's due to her personal pedagogic experiences of being made to stand in a trash can in the first grade by her teacher, instructors hitting you with rulers, and being made to pay money to the church if you dropped your pencil.

I'll end my whinge with a more hopeful and dreamy Proust quote, which she references a smidge past the center of the book on page 86 -
	"he said it was his idea of earthly happiness: “To live in contact with those I love, with the beauties of nature, with a quantity of books and music, and to have, within easy distance, a French theater.”
    * Nice Proust, throw some games in there, and I'd say we've got it pretty good.
