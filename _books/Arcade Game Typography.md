"Limitation is a great driver of creativity, as is technological innocence." (11)

Digital games are not primarily living in our tangible space, and to adapt to this, they are experienced through a windowed ergodic nature, and we use visual shorthand to indicate the status of the world (e.g. a health bar, an inventory, the amount of success chance for an action). These ludonarrative worlds have rules that exist in a vacuum of their own diagesis and interface programming. Most often, all of these things rely on text. Whether the type is narrative exposition for dialogue and journals, or simply status indication, such as the score in pong.

Arcade Game Typography by Toshi Omagari covers almost exclusively 8x8 fonts from the start of the arcade genre of the 1970s, all the way to the early 2000s. Though when we say 8x8 pixel bitmaps for each font glyph, designs are more often dealing with 7x7 pixel-wise, as Omagari notes, because you wouldn't want characters to bleed together. Despite this considerably constrained creative space, there are surprisingly diverse results of expression and style, especially so when color expands the initial 1 bit pallet, and expanded possibilities become tangible, like drop shadows.

Pixel fonts naturally fell out of fashion given the rise of computational resolution capabilities and expectations, only to come back into fashion for nostalgia purposes and the occasional restrictive technology like early Game Boys. You can look at such 8x8 staples like the **Atari** font, which is essentially the Helvetica of video game fonts; it was so popular that it was used for everything from Pac-Man to Donkey Kong to Mario Bros. to The Legend of Zelda, and has even made it into modern games like 2014s Shovel Knight in a fit of nostalgic reference (45).

Similarly, to the book <a href="https://oddopuscomposer.github.io/books/Thinking%20with%20Type/">Designing for Text</a> by Ellen Lupton, Omagari also has an elegant description for the differences between font and typeface -  "A typeface is an abstract idea, a design that does not take any physical form, while a font is a physical manifestation of a typeface."(page 12). Though note, people seem to use these terms often interchangeably today. Another quote I like from him to start off the book is "(he) discovered mundane typefaces that were elevated by the contexts in which they appear" (page 9) when talking about his research experience for this book.

Overall, it is an enjoyable romp through the 8x8 hyperfixation of fonts in old arcade games that go surprisingly well. I learned some interesting lore and graphic design tidbits, and even developed a quirky list of 10 wonky games to try out that I was unaware of.
