<b>General Thoughts</b>:
This is a nice collective rundown of artists and professionals who express their art through personal mapping and infographics. In all honesty, I found the book intriguing, though not entirely inspirational. The introduction had the most to chew on,  and I was surprised mot not to find some of my favourite experimental mapmakers illuminated in the book's pages. Overall, the whole book reads quite quickly, as it is rather lite on text, and is beautifully laid out with lovely hardback cloth-covered binding. Despite this, there are occasionally awkward text block placements and justification, which felt intentional but not well thought out in its execution for readability. The overall work resembles much more a concise gallery or curator catalogue of works, which makes sense as the acknowledgements reveal the book grew from a gallery map marathon held at Serpentine Gallery London.

My most prominent contemplations emerged around the bulk of the text in Tom McCarthy's Introduction and Hans Ulrich Obrist closing statement.

McCarthy writes, "Maps are not copies; they are projections, 'means' (Steers again) 'of representing the lines of latitude and longitude of the globe on a flat sheet of paper.'" (referencing J.A. Steers, Introduction to the Study of Map Projections 1927) (p. 7). Projection is not neutral or natural; it is given, not inherent truth. Obrist's own words mirror this thought well: "maps produce new realities much as they seek to document current ones", and "Maps are errors to arrive at truth" (p. 233)

Mapping is an art that attempts to obtain truth and relate truths to one another to a degree. It is thorough, ever morphing and with endless gaps forming and shifting. Consider Lewis Carroll's perfect blank map of the Bellman's Ocean Chart from Hunting of the Snark,  1:1 map ratio blocking the sun from Sylvie and Bruno Concluded, or perhaps or Jorge Luis Borges' 1:1, immediately becoming inaccurate and useless from On Exactitude in Science.

All in all, though this book did not have the relevance I was looking for, I did enjoy learning two new terms: psychogeography and dérive.

**Psychogeography**:  The study of the specific effects of the geographical environment (whether consciously organised or not) on the emotions and behaviour of individuals", as defined by Internationale Situationniste #1 (June 1958, translated by Ken Knabb)
In practice, it is a way of engaging with the landscape as both subjective and objective space, aiming to subvert, misuse, or even reveal the affordances through which the social and economic order is reproduced. The psychogeographer treats the city as an urban adventure, navigating in an anti-capitalist manner by way of ignoring signs and common routes designed to make/spend money or earn rewards. It is an art of exploration and appreciation, and sometimes I just want to walk, you know?

**Dérive**: is a psychogeographic concept. An unplanned journey through a landscape, often urban, in which participants take a break from focusing on their everyday relations to their social environment (you can read more about these concepts in Guy Debord's Theory of the Dérive).

I admit this wanderlust is intriguing to me. I am all for Marxist ambience praxis of urban non-capitalist wanderings. Plus, it seems potentially linked to the concepts of queer time and space, which is inherently anti-capitalist, bending away from Elizabeth Freeman's Chrononormativity and leaning into self-definition to establish desires and paths in the wake of heteronormative patriarchal failure, à la Jack Halberstam.
It's also interesting when thinking of the simulation algorithms for progression, isolation, and decay of procedural world emergence and understood knowledge. A quote from Raoul Vaneigem, in conversation with Obrist, kind of blends these duelling interests and thoughts of mine: "The dérive is not merely a spatiotemporal drift through urban landscapes, but a drift through the spaces of the imagination in order to arrive at an invention of reality" (p. 233). 

Lastly, I will share the map poem/instruction by Golden+Senneby titled Map Piece 1962 Summer (p. 77), as I think this also exemplifies the dérive and just sounds generally like an interesting exercise.
